Appliance Leak Water Damage Help in Franklin

Three appliances cause most of the leaks we see. Washer supply hoses can burst and keep spraying at full pressure until the valve is closed. A dishwasher tends to leak slowly from a door seal or drain hose, out of sight under the cabinets. A refrigerator ice maker line is a thin tube that can drip behind the unit for months. Cleanup is different for each of them.

On every Franklin job, the first step is moving the appliance and tracing the path the water took. In kitchens that usually means under the cabinets and beneath the flooring. With a laundry leak we look at the rooms next to it and the wall behind the washer, plus the ceiling below when the laundry sits on an upper floor. Standing water is extracted, absorbed moisture is measured, and drying equipment is sized to fit the wet area.

Do This First

Your First Steps

  1. Shut the Machine DownStop the cycle and unplug the machine if the outlet and floor around it are dry. When either one is wet, turn the power off at the breaker.
  2. Shut Off the Water LineFor a washing machine, close both the cold and hot valves on the wall at its back. Dishwashers shut off under the sink. Refrigerator lines have a small valve behind or below.
  3. Soak Up Standing WaterUse towels or a mop on whatever water is within reach, which keeps it from spreading beneath flooring and cabinets. Do not vacuum unless the machine is rated for wet and dry use.
  4. Keep the Door ClosedA dishwasher or washer that still holds water should stay closed. The entire load of water may spill across the floor if the door is opened mid-cycle.
  5. Photograph the Failed PartTake pictures of the hose, valve or fitting that failed and keep the part. Both the appliance maker and your insurer may want to look at it.
Insurance

Does Insurance Cover It?

When a machine overflows unexpectedly or a hose bursts, that is typically treated as sudden and accidental, which many homeowners policies cover. Payment typically goes toward the damage the water did, and the appliance or the part that failed is not included. Gradual leaks usually fall outside coverage, so slow seepage at a dishwasher or an ice maker line makes for a tougher claim. Some insurers also ask about the age and upkeep of the hose. Check your policy, keep the failed part and take photos before cleanup.

Why It Happens

What Causes It

  • A failed water inlet valve that keeps filling the washer or dishwasher after it should stop.
  • An overflow caused by an overloaded drum or by using too much detergent.
  • A kinked or cracked ice maker line, or one whose fitting has worked loose.
  • Poor installation, such as a dishwasher set out of level or a supply fitting not fully tightened.
  • Dishwasher parts that wear out, including the door gasket, the drain hose and the seal on the pump.

What the Water Damages

Baseboards and Wall Bottoms

Drywall edges and baseboards pull up water that stands on a kitchen or laundry room floor. Since it is out of view, the wall behind the machine tends to be wetter than the rest.

Mold Behind Appliances

Behind a refrigerator or a dishwasher it is warm and dark, and nobody checks there often. A slow drip there feeds mold on the wall and floor long before anyone notices.

Kitchen Cabinets

From below, a leaking dishwasher wets the toe kicks and the side panels of the cabinets. The panels swell and separate, and wet cabinet boxes can hold moisture against the wall for weeks.

Downstairs Ceiling Damage

In a home with an upstairs laundry, leak water passes through the floor and pools on the ceiling underneath. Drywall there sags and stains while the insulation over it holds water.
